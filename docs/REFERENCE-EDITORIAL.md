# Editing from a reference

The travel cuts prompted this review because they felt like automatic photo recaps. They had a destination, selected highlights, a score and transitions. Most cuts simply introduced another attractive view. The reference study examined how an edit can give those changes a purpose.

## Start with what the viewer follows

Write one sentence that describes what the viewer sees change. “A boat slips under a rock ceiling and daylight gives way to darkness” gives you a sequence to find in the footage. “Best shots from the trip” gives you a selection. A trip can support either, but the first has an event with a setup and payoff.

- **Give the sequence an anchor.** A person in the same part of the frame, a recurring object, or a direction of movement can make different locations feel continuous. In one travel reel, the same low camera angle and centered pose made each ceiling a reveal. Another used a recurring car and its passengers to keep the city connected to their experience.
- **Plan the transition around the source.** A spin match needs two shots turning in the same direction with compatible framing. A hand-cover cut needs a convincingly covered frame at the join. A visual loop needs a final action or composition that reconnects to the opening. Write down what needs to match before trying an effect. If the archive lacks the pair, choose a sequence the footage can support or capture the missing shot.
- **Use timing to change the viewer’s expectation.** The extreme-sports example spends nearly three seconds on the wind-up before changes arrive roughly every 0.27 seconds. The triathlon reel opens with roughly 1.5-second intervals, then moves into changes about every 0.4 seconds as the action starts. The held setups are part of what makes the faster sections feel fast. These are measurements from particular reels.
- **Give sound a job.** Mark where an action, a reveal or a change in space should sound different. Keys, a footstep, the slap of a paddle or the shift in ambience under a rock ceiling can carry a cut. A score can set up an accent or sustain a hold. Listen to decide whether a peak in the final mix is a beat, a voice or an effect before attributing an edit to it.
- **Keep effects tied to a visible cause.** In the masked loop, a hand gesture triggers animated cracks and a burst. In the first-person visual-effects reel, red energy changes what happens in the scene before the edit reveals its source. These effects carry information the viewer needs. Put the strongest treatment where it changes the scene or delivers the payoff.
- **Let light and framing connect the shots.** Repeated low angles and green practical lights made separate night locations feel like one scene. The warm storefronts and framed street views of a grading tutorial already shared visual qualities before the grade. Review light, color and subject placement across adjacent shots before choosing an overall treatment.
- **Let a single setup carry the piece when it can.** A group portrait that comes to life, an overhead reaction with a caption, and a behind-the-scenes setup followed by its result all work with few cuts. Hold long enough for the viewer to notice the change.

## Plan a few shots before you film

Decide what the viewer should experience, then capture the few photos or clips that make it possible. A one-sentence idea and a short shot list are enough. Keep room for spontaneous footage.

**Singapore example · One turn takes me through the city**

1. Film the same turn at three landmarks. Use the same camera height and similar framing. Keep yourself in roughly the same place in the frame and turn in the same direction at each stop.
2. Record at least a second before and after each turn so you have room to choose the cut. Compare the takes on location and repeat one if the pose or direction differs.
3. In Edits, join the clips at the same point mid-turn. The body movement continues while the location changes.

**Vietnam example · A boat passes from daylight into a cave**

1. Show the cave ahead so the viewer knows where the boat is going.
2. Hold as the rock ceiling passes overhead. Leave enough of the approach in the shot for the drop in light to register.
3. Show the first glimpse from inside. Record a few seconds of natural sound on either side of the entrance so the mix can convey the change in space.

With footage you already have, look for an action that contains its own setup and payoff. The Vietnam boat footage already has that shape. The Singapore turn depends on capturing a matching set.

## From reference to timeline

1. **Watch for the idea.** Write down the opening, the moment the premise becomes clear, and what the ending does. Identify the anchor that stays recognizable when the location, scale or treatment changes.
2. **Inspect the decisive moment.** Run `footagepilot reference` on a local copy, or inspect signed-in playback when a local copy is unavailable. Step through the frames before and after the change. Record approximate timestamps, where the subject sits, what direction anything moves, and whether the cut coincides with a visible action or audio transient. [Reference timing](REFERENCE-TIMING.md)
3. **Test the archive against the idea.** List the candidate shot for the setup, the action, and its reaction or result. For a matched transition, compare the actual end and beginning frames. For a reveal, check that the footage contains both a before and an after. Keep missing footage visible in the plan.
4. **Build the rough cut around the decisive moment.** Set its in/out points and ending first. Establish the necessary context, then audition ambience, effects and music at the intended changes. Keep short and long holds where each serves a purpose.
5. **Review the export against the idea.** Watch once for whether the premise and payoff read, then inspect the transition frames and listen to the sound. Compare with the previous version. Record a technical pass separately from the viewer’s reaction.

A useful note per sequence is: **idea, anchor, setup shot, payoff shot, intended sound cue, evidence, and missing coverage**. Preserve timing uncertainty when working from frame samples.

## Applying the study to travel footage

| Available material | Editorial approach | What to check |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Boat footage moving from open water to a low cave ceiling | Make the passage the event. Use the low boat perspective, hold as the ceiling enters, and let the interior change the sound and framing. | Verify the entrance and interior connect spatially. Check for usable source ambience before choosing the mix. |
| Waterfront, architecture and night photos | Build around a visible motif, such as reflections or a repeated horizon line, and choose a closing frame that pays it off. | Compare the candidates side by side. Check that the motif reads at phone size. |
| New footage at several landmarks | Film a repeated turn, pose or camera move with consistent framing, direction and usable start/end handles. | Check the pairs on location. The reviewed archive has no verified matching set for the landmark gesture idea. |

## Evidence and local retention

The study covered all 30 visible reels across two collections. Twenty-eight full local copies were measured and reviewed with timed frame samples. Two additional reels were inspected through timestamped frames in signed-in playback. One local copy had no audio track. The constant-pulse estimator returned a candidate in two local copies. Proximity to an audio transient does not establish beat sync or artistic intent. A scene detector can fire on flashes and overlay changes as well as cuts.
